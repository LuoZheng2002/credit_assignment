    EXPLOITING TREE STRUCTURE FOR CREDIT ASSIGNMENT IN RL TRAINING OF LLMS

*Keywords:* LLM, Reasoning, Credit Assignment, Reinforcement Learning
*TL;DR:* TEMPO builds a prefix tree from grouped responses, uses nonparametric prefix values, and adds branch-gated TD to GRPO—enabling token-level credit without a value net and faster, higher-accuracy training on math and medical benchmarks.
*Abstract:*

Reinforcement learning improves LLM reasoning, yet sparse delayed reward
over long sequences makes token-level credit assignment the key
bottleneck. We study the verifiable-reward setting, where the final
answer is checkable and multiple responses can be drawn per prompt.
Reasoning tasks in math and medical QA align with this setup, where only
a few decision tokens significantly impact the outcome. PPO offers
token-level advantages with a learned value model, but it is complex to
train both the actor and critic models simultaneously, and it is not
easily generalizable, as the token-level values from the critic model
can make training prone to overfitting. GRPO is critic-free and supports
verifiable rewards, but spreads a single sequence-level return across
tokens and ignores branching. We introduce Prefix-to-Tree (P2T), a
simple procedure that converts a group of responses into a prefix tree
and computes nonparametric prefix values V(s) by aggregating descendant
outcomes. Built on P2T, we propose TEMPO (Tree-Estimated Mean Prefix
Value for Policy Optimization), a critic-free algorithm that augments
the group-relative outcome signal of GRPO with branch-gated temporal-
difference corrections derived from the tree. At non-branch tokens, the
temporal-difference (TD) term is zero, so TEMPO reduces to GRPO; at
branching tokens, it supplies precise token-level credit without a
learned value network or extra judges/teachers. On Qwen3-1.7B/4B, TEMPO
outperforms PPO and GRPO on in-distribution (MATH, MedQA) and out-of-
distribution (GSM-HARD, AMC23, MedMCQA, MMLU-Medical) benchmarks, and
reaches higher validation accuracy with less wall-clock time.

*Summary:*

This paper tackles the token-level credit assignment challenge in
reinforcement learning for large language models with verifiable rewards
(e.g., math or medical reasoning). The authors propose TEMPO. TEMPO is a
critic-free method that constructs a prefix tree from multiple sampled
responses per prompt, computes nonparametric prefix values, and
introduces branch-aware corrections. TEMPO provides fine-grained credit
at branching tokens while preserving the efficiency and simplicity of
GRPO-style training. Empirical results on MATH, MedQA, and several out-
of-distribution benchmarks show that TEMPO outperforms PPO, GRPO, and
HEPO in both convergence speed and accuracy using Qwen3-1.7B/4B models.

*Soundness:* 2: fair
*Presentation:* 3: good
*Contribution:* 2: fair
*Strengths:*

 1.

    The studied problem is important. Sparse reward is a key limitation
    of current RL for LLMs, which can cause sub-optimal optimization.

 2.

    The proposed idea is clear and easy to understand. Using a tree to
    calculate a more accurate reward for each token can be a good way to
    introduce such dense rewards.

 3.

    The writing is clear and easy to understand.

*Weaknesses:*

 1.

    The proposed method does not explicitly control the branch of the
    repeated sampling tree, but relies on implicit branching across
    sampled responses. In many prompts, only a small portion of tokens
    can actually form branch points, so most updates may reduce to GRPO
    with sparse rewards. Some further analysis can be conducted to
    verify why implicitly building the repeated sampling tree is a good
    choice and introduces enough dense reward signals.

 2.

    Another concern is related to the experiment setting. As illustrated
    in Table 2, the maximum response length is only around 1k tokens,
    which is far shorter than the original model’s typical response
    length (up to 40k). Such a limited sequence length makes it
    difficult to fully justify the training effectiveness of the
    proposed method, as long-horizon reasoning and delayed credit
    assignment are precisely where token-level advantages should matter
    most. Moreover, this limitation is closely tied to the previous
    point on implicit branching; when the response length is long, the
    later tokens may not share any branches with other responses, which
    makes the reward very sparse.

 3.

    The training step is also limited. Usually, in recent RL papers, >
    800 training iterations are needed to make sure training converges.
    However, the number of training iterations on Math is 220, which can
    be too few. Despite that I feel that the overall idea is neat, but
    the evaluation part is not enough to support the major hypothesis of
    the paper.

*Questions:*

See weakness.

*Comment:*

Thanks for the thoughtful and constructive feedback. We address each
point below and have updated the draft accordingly.

1: Implicit branching vs. dense signals. Our method intentionally uses
the implicit prefix tree induced by repeated sampling, avoiding extra
samplers and retaining GRPO’s drop-in loop. We now include TREERPO—which
performs explicit tree sampling to densify credit—as a baseline. Under
identical hardware, our method still converges faster and to higher
accuracy (see Fig. 4), while explicit tree sampling incurs additional
rollout overhead. Empirically, the TD term in our advantages fires
exactly where decisions occur: branching tokens concentrate at strategy/
diagnosis pivots (Fig. 6), and higher initial branch counts predict
faster learning and higher final accuracy (Fig. 7). An ablation shows
that (i) prefix-to-tree values (P2T) already help over GRPO and (ii)
adding branch-gated TD yields the full gain (Table 2). Together, these
analyses support that implicit trees provide sufficiently informative
(and well-targeted) token-level signals without the sampling cost of
explicit tree construction.

2: Sequence length clarification. We apologize for the confusion. The 1k
limit is the maximum prompt length, not the response length. Our
experiments use 8192 as the maximum response length, which we have
clarified in the revision Table 3. Figure 8 reports mean response length
across training on both domains, showing that 8k comfortably covers our
trajectories in practice; thus the long-horizon, delayed-credit regime
is exercised under our settings.

3: Training iterations and convergence. To make the training more
stable, we train with a large effective batch (512 samples), which
reduces the number of outer iterations while maintaining total episode
budget. We added the information to Table 3 of the revision draft.
Validation curves indicate clear convergence under our schedule; adding
more iterations leads to overfitting (training reward increase,
validation reward stagnant or decrease), as shown in Figure 9.

We appreciate the reviewer’s suggestions. We hope these updates address
the concerns; we’re happy to incorporate any further suggestions.

*Summary:*

This paper claims that by grouping the shared prefixes among a group of
responses to a query when doing GRPO, one can extract fine-grained
credit assignment. They show that this leads to gains in mathematical
and medical tasks claiming this proves its generality. They analyze the
branching tokens and the effect of the size of the group on the results
providing further intuition.

*Soundness:* 4: excellent
*Presentation:* 4: excellent
*Contribution:* 2: fair
*Strengths:*

I think the paper correctly claims that credit assignment is troublesome
in current reasoning models. Exploiting the shared prefixes, if they
exists, is a smart way to extract fine-grained credit assignment. The
paper really does go deep in analyzing this behaviour and provides
insights. It does a thorough related work section.

*Weaknesses:*

The problem with the work is that, it does not lead to significantly
better credit assignment. The main problem is, the responses from a
diverse reasoning model quickly diverge from each other and therefore,
aside from the maybe first few thousand tokens, the rest will only have
one response. It is very intuitive that this happens: the paths simply
diverge due to sampling of tokens. This is very limiting. I have
confidence that what authors have shown really holds: the extraction of
the extra juice left in the shared prefixes for more accurate credit
assignment is useful. But, the problem is that, the effects stop there.
It cannot substantially improve: there is no knob to extract finer
credit assignment. This paper can be a nice addition to a GRPO run, but
it is not a significant contribution: it has small impact.

Aside from the above, I am wondering why the baseline numbers reported
for MATH500 are different from Qwen3 official report. In theirs, the
Qwen3-4B performance is 97% on MATH500 in thinking mode. However, the
authors train and it starts from 82 and goes to 87% on validation. This
seems contradictory to me and indicates maybe the authors tested their
method on non-thinking mode. I think this is the case especially as the
authors set the maximum response length to 1024 tokens: this is very low
for reasoning models. I think if the authors tested their method on the
correct setup, with high number of max responses tokens and in thinking
mode, their method would have significant trouble because aside from the
the first thousand tokens, the rest will only have just one response due
to diverging branches.

Unfortunately, these two weaknesses leaves me with no choice but to
reject the paper. I think the results of the paper hold and are correct,
but does not scale to actual training runs with long reasoning chains.

*Questions:*

1-Are you using the thinking mode of the said models or their non-
thinking mode? 2-If you set the maximum response length to a standard
number, preferably 32K, if compute is limited 8K, would the results hold?

*Comment:*

Thank you for your thoughtful review. We address each point and clarify
our setup below; we’ve also updated the draft accordingly.

1: “Implicit branching gives only small gains; paths quickly diverge;
there’s no knob for finer credit.” Our premise is that most outcome-
moving decisions concentrate at early/mid “branch tokens,” not uniformly
across the whole sequence. This matches our diagnostics: Where the TD
fires. Branch tokens (high-entropy forks) localize at strategy/diagnosis
pivots; non-branch tokens receive zero TD and we safely fall back to
GRPO (no harm, no noise injection).

Does implicit branching suffice? Yes. We added TREERPO (explicit tree
sampling with denser step signals) as a baseline and TEMPO still
outperforms it in accuracy with lower wall-clock time, because explicit
tree sampling demands substantially more rollouts. This supports our
hypothesis: precise credit at the few key tokens is sufficient; dense
credit everywhere is not required in practice.

“No knob” concern. While we don’t require a special sampler, there are
controllable levers that increase shared prefixes (and thus TD
opportunities): group size G, (in our ablations) the observed branch
budget correlates with faster learning. Our ablation study (Table 2)
shows incremental, robust gains. In short, TEMPO is more than a “nice
add-on”: it offers targeted token-level TD with GRPO-like simplicity and
cost, and scales better than explicit tree-sampling methods under
matched compute.

2: “1k max length; long-horizon chains not exercised; maybe non-thinking
mode.” We apologize for the confusion. 1k refers to the prompt max
length, not the response. Our max response length is 8192; we corrected
Table 3 in the revision and added Fig. 8 (mean response length over
training) to show 8k comfortably covers our trajectories. We also use
the model’s thinking mode, not the no-think mode.

Clarifications on datasets:

MATH vs. MATH500: Our math experiments use the MATH benchmark of
Hendrycks et al, not MATH500. MATH has both training and test subset and
that's the main reason why we choose to experiment with. The 97% figure
cited for Qwen3-4B is for MATH500 in “thinking” mode, a different
dataset. Our numbers aren’t contradictory; they target MATH under RL
training, not one-shot eval on MATH500.

Measuring mathematical problem solving with the math dataset(Dan
Hendrycks, Collin Burns, Saurav Kadavath, Akul Arora, Steven Basart,
Eric Tang, Dawn Song, and Jacob Steinhardt)

Would results hold with 32k (or 8k if limited)?: We already train with
8k responses; Fig. 8 shows it is sufficient for the chains we generate.

We hope these updates address the concerns and we welcome any further
suggestions.

*Comment:*

Thank you for the thoughtful review and for emphasizing comparisons to
tree-based methods. We’ve revised the paper to address novelty,
evaluation breadth, and clarity.

On novelty vs. TREERPO / “incremental” framing. Our method differs
conceptually and operationally from TREERPO and related tree-sampling
approaches:

No explicit tree sampler. We use the implicit prefix tree naturally
induced by the G responses per prompt (standard GRPO sampling). We do
not branch via a new sampler nor require process rewards or step-wise
teachers.

Nonparametric prefix value + branch-gated TD. We compute a critic-free,
nonparametric prefix value from all descendants sharing then add a TD
correction only at branch tokens; at non-branch tokens the TD term is
zero and the update reduces to GRPO.

GRPO-compatible, same loop/cost. Unlike tree-sampling methods that
expand trajectories to densify rewards, our updates stay in the GRPO
loop with the same sampling budget and lower wall-clock. These choices
target a different point on the accuracy–efficiency frontier: tree-
aware, token-level credit without extra sampling or learned critics.

On evaluation breadth (TREERPO). We have added TREERPO as a baseline.
Under matched hardware and sampling budgets, our method attains higher
validation accuracy with less wall-clock (new Fig. 4). This strengthens
the claim that implicit-tree TD credit is a competitive (and efficient)
alternative to explicit tree sampling.

Reproducibility note. The TREERPO paper links to a GitHub repo that
appears to correspond to a different project; we could not locate an
official TREERPO implementation. We therefore re-implemented TREERPO
from the paper to the best of our understanding and verified settings
carefully. While we aimed for fidelity, our implementation may not
reflect the authors’ system exactly. We will open-source our
reimplementation and configs for transparency.

Responses to questions

Q1. How/when is branching created? We do not force splits. Given G
rollouts per prompt, we build the implicit prefix tree by aligning token
positions across the G responses. We compute the nonparametric V(s_t)
from the subtree and apply TD only at these branch tokens; else where
the TD term is zero (falls back to GRPO). This happens every training
step for each prompt’s group.

Q2. “Fixed number of branches per prompt—should be adaptive.” We do not
fix the number of branches; it is induced by the data (G, temperature/
top-p, task). In ablations we stratify by observed branch count to study
learning dynamics (not to fix it). Practically, adaptivity comes from
the sampling distribution: higher temperature or larger G yields more
branching—and thus more TD opportunities—without changing the algorithm.

Q3. Add comparisons against other tree-based approaches (TREERPO). Done.

We now include TREERPO using authors’ settings where possible. Our
approach outperforms TREERPO with lower wall-clock under matched budgets
(Fig. 4), indicating that dense step-signals via explicit branching are
not required to realize strong gains.

Q4. Provide ablations to isolate contributions. Added.

We include an ablation study in Appendix Table 2.

We hope those clarifications and revisions addressed the reviewer's
concern. Please let us know if there's any further comments.

*Comment:*

Thanks for the careful read and concrete pointers. We address each point
and have updated the draft accordingly.

 1. Novelty vs. TreeRPO We agree TreeRPO is a closely related, tree-
    based line; our contribution targets a different point on the
    accuracy–efficiency frontier:

Implicit vs. explicit trees. TEMPO builds the implicit prefix tree from
the standard GRPO group of rollouts—no new sampler, no step-wise reward/
judge. TREERPO performs explicit tree sampling/search to densify step
signals.

Critic-free, branch-gated TD. TEMPO computes a nonparametric prefix
value V(s_t) from descendants and adds TD only at branch tokens; non-
branch tokens revert exactly to GRPO (stable, zero-TD).

Same loop/cost as GRPO. TEMPO keeps GRPO’s training loop and sampling
budget; explicit tree search typically incurs higher rollout cost.

 2.

    Missing TREERPO baseline We have added TreeRPO to the main results.
    Under matched hardware and sampling budgets, TEMPO attains higher
    validation accuracy with less wall-clock time. (We note the TreeRPO
    paper’s GitHub link appears to point to a different project; we
    therefore re-implemented TREERPO from the paper to the best of our
    understanding and will release our reproduction and configs. Results
    may not identically match the authors’ private code.)

 3.

    Justification for the advantage normalization (line 299) Our
    advantage has two terms: a group-normalized outcome signal (GRPO’s
    Monte-Carlo, response-level) and a branch-gated TD correction
    (token-level). We standardize the MC term in the usual GRPO way
    using the group mean/std. For the TD term, we scale by the same
    group std. The motivation is simple:

Balance of signals. We want equal a priori importance of response-level
(MC) and token-level (TD) signals on a comparable numeric scale. Using
the same group std for both avoids the TD term dominating (or vanishing)
when its own sample variance is small/unstable at early training or when
branching is sparse.

comments/suggestions.
